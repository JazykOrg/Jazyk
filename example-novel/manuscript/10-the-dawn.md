# The dawn

The first light came over the water and Ines was still in the Lantern Room, the lantern
burning low, the kettle long since cold. She had kept the light for the crew through the
night, and now the crew was gone and the room was hers and the wall was holding.

She went to the window. The north face of the wall was braced from top to bottom, the
timber and the wedges and the tarred rope catching the grey light, and at the crack the
work Theo had called through the night stood in the dawn like a thing that had been
argued for and won. She had watched him call the braces from the quay, had seen him stand
at the crack when the wind dropped to a moan and look up at the Lantern Room, and she had
seen the way he had not left it, the way he had stayed at the crack until the last brace
took.

She set the kettle on the stove again and did not know why. The crew would not come in
again, and the kettle was for the crew, and yet she set it on, and she stood at the stove
and watched the water and the watching was the thing that told her, before she had a word
for it, that the thing she had felt for him through the night was not the thing she had
felt for him the night before. Through the night she had felt for him the way she felt for
the crew, the way she felt for the wall, the way she felt for the town that kept the
lantern lit. Now she felt for him the way she felt for the room she kept, the way she felt
for the lease that tied the frontage to the wall, the way she felt for the thing that was
hers and that she would not give up.

She did not name it. She set the kettle on and she watched the water and she stood at the
window and watched the braced wall, and the watching was the change, and the change was the
thing the reader could see in the doing of it, in the kettle set on for no one, in the
standing at the window, in the way she would not leave the room.

It was Ines who changed in this scene, and only Ines. Theo was at the wall, and Theo had
not changed, and the scene stayed in Ines's head and reported only what Ines saw and heard
and thought, and the change that happened here was hers alone.

Later, when the light was full and the wall stood in it, Ines went to the door of the
Lantern Room and stood in the doorway and looked out at the quay, and she said to herself,
quietly, that she loved him. The saying was the admission, and the admission came after the
kettle and after the window and after the watching, and the change had been visible to the
reader in the doing of it before Ines had a word for it.
