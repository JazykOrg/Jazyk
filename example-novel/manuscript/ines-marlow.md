# Ines Marlow

Ines Marlow was thirty-one years old, and her occupation was bookseller. She was
introduced by her age and her occupation first, and then by what she carried into the
story: the Lantern Room, the lease, the family, and the wall.

## The Lantern Room

Ines owned and ran the Lantern Room, the bookshop on the quay, and she had done so since
her mother died four years before the book opened. The Lantern Room had two floors: the
shop on the ground floor and a single room above it, which Ines let to lodgers when she
needed the money. The takings ledger lived under the counter, where Ines wrote the day's
takings in pencil, in the same ledger her grandmother had used.

Ines kept a pencil behind her ear, and she wrote the day's takings in the same ledger her
grandmother used, the ledger that had passed down the family the way a key passes.

Ines read standing up. She did not sit to read, and she did not lie down to read; she
stood at the counter or in the back room, and she read standing up, the way she had
always read.

Ines never called the shop "the bookshop." To her it was the shop, or the Lantern Room,
and the phrase "the bookshop" was not hers, and she did not use it.

## The family

Ines was a member of the Marlow family, and she was the last of it still living in Port
Alder. The Marlow family was Ines's family, and Ines was the only Marlow who appeared in
the book; her grandmother and her mother were spoken of but never seen. The grandmother
had signed the lease in 1961, and the mother had kept the room between the grandmother and
Ines, and the two of them were names and memories, and Ines was the one who stood in the
room and lit the lantern and kept the kettle on.

Ines was born in Port Alder, and she had never lived anywhere else. Port Alder was her
birthplace, and no other place was a former home, and the town was the whole of her
life, and the harbor was the whole of her world.

## The name

Dot had called Ines Nessa since Ines was six, and Callum had picked the name up at school.
Nobody else used the name, and Ines did not answer to it from strangers. The name was the
name of the people who had known her since she was small, and a stranger who called her
Nessa was a stranger who did not know her, and Ines did not answer to a name that was not
hers from the people who had given it to her.

## Temperament

Ines was quick to anger and slow to admit she was wrong, and the book made her do both.
She got angry quickly, and she held the anger, and she admitted she was wrong slowly, and
the admitting was the thing the book made her do, and the anger was the thing she did
first.

## The arc

Ines was thirty-one, and she had never had a reason to leave Port Alder. The arc gave her
one, in the shape of Theo's next contract, and she declined it. The contract was the
reason to leave, and the declining was the thing she did, and the staying was the thing
the arc made her do.

Ines and Theo had never heard of each other before chapter 1. They did not know each
other's names, and they did not know each other's histories, and the meeting was the
thing that happened in the book, and the not-knowing was the thing that was true before
it.

Ines's stance toward Theo changed four times. She began as a stranger, and she became an
acquaintance when Theo took the upstairs room, and she became a rival when Dot told her
who Theo worked for, and she became an ally when Theo offered her the crack finding, and
she became beloved when Theo shored up the wall through the storm. The rival stretch was
the long one, from chapter 3 to chapter 5, and it was where Ines was wrong about Theo for
reasons that were entirely his fault.

Ines and Theo both started as strangers, and both ended as beloved, and beloved was where
the book left them, and nothing moved either lead out of it. Neither Ines nor Theo ever
moved back down a stance.

## The scenes

In chapter 1, Ines let the upstairs room to Theo without asking what brought him to town.
She let the room, and she did not ask, and the not-asking was the thing she did, and the
room was the thing she let, and Theo was the one who took it.

In chapter 3, Dot told Nessa who Theo worked for, and Ines asked Dot for the harbor
ledger, the book in the ferry office safe that recorded every lease on the quay. Ines
found the Marlow lease in the harbor ledger and read the clause that granted the frontage
for as long as the harbor wall stood, and she decided to take the lease to the harbor
council and block the demolition.

In chapter 4, Ines confronted Theo on the quay about the survey and asked him what he
found. Theo refused to show Ines the report and told her to ask Callum.

In chapter 5, Callum asked Nessa to drop the petition, for old times' sake. Ines
presented the harbor ledger to the harbor council to block the demolition, and when she
opened the ledger at the meeting the lease page had been cut out, and the council voted to
proceed with the demolition. Ines walked into the council believing Theo was her enemy,
and she did not yet know that Theo had decided she should know about the crack.

In chapter 6, Ines kept the lantern lit and the kettle on for the harbor crew until dawn.

In chapter 7, Ines found Theo's field notebook in the upstairs room and read the
measurements he had written down twice. In the notebook scene, Ines wanted to put Theo's
notebook back in his bag before he noticed it was out, and she kept it, and she read it,
and she took it to the council. Ines gave the notebook to the harbor council as evidence
that the crack was known and left out of the report.

In chapter 8, Ines asked Theo to stay, and he told her he already had.

## Point of view

Ines carried the point of view. The odd chapters belonged to Ines, and chapter 1 opened in
Ines's head watching the ferry come in. The notebook scene's point of view was Ines, and
the reader saw the scene through Ines's head. Ines and Theo carried the point of view,
and Callum and Dot were seen only through them, and what Callum and Dot thought was shown
only through what Ines or Theo saw them do.

## The love

Ines and Theo fell in love over the fight for the wall. The fight was the thing they fell
in love over, and the wall was the thing they fought for, and the love was the thing the
fight made, and the two of them were the ones who fell in love, and the wall was the
thing they did it over.
